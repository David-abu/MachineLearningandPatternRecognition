\section*{Question 1: MLP approximation of class posteriors}

\subsection*{Data distribution}
There are $C=4$ classes with uniform priors $P(L=c)=1/4$ and Gaussian class-conditional pdfs $p(\x|L=c)=\mathcal N(\x;\bm\mu_c,\Sigma_c)$, $\x\in\mathbb R^3$. The means sit on the vertices of a regular tetrahedron scaled by $s=0.85$:
\[
\bm\mu_1=s[1,1,1]^T,\quad \bm\mu_2=s[1,-1,-1]^T,\quad \bm\mu_3=s[-1,1,-1]^T,\quad \bm\mu_4=s[-1,-1,1]^T .
\]
Each covariance is $\Sigma_c=R_c\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm\lambda_c)\,R_c^T$ with a different rotation $R_c$ and eigenvalues
$\bm\lambda_1=(1.0,0.6,0.3)$, $\bm\lambda_2=(0.8,0.8,0.4)$, $\bm\lambda_3=(1.2,0.5,0.5)$, $\bm\lambda_4=(0.7,0.6,0.9)$, so the classes have different shapes and orientations and the optimal decision boundaries are quadratic. The rotation angles are listed in \texttt{q1.py}. The scale $s$ was tuned by Monte Carlo (200{,}000 samples per candidate): $s=0.8,0.9,1.0$ gave Bayes errors of $17.2\%$, $12.8\%$ and $9.1\%$, so $s=0.85$ places the optimal error near the middle of the required 10--20\% range.

Training sets with $N\in\{100,500,1000,5000,10000\}$ samples and a test set with 100{,}000 samples were drawn independently. The test set was used only for final performance evaluation. Its class counts are 24973, 25152, 25003, 24872.

\subsection*{Theoretically optimal classifier}
With 0--1 loss, the minimum-expected-risk rule is the MAP rule
\[
D(\x)=\argmax_{c}\;P(L=c|\x)=\argmax_c\;\Big[\ln\mathcal N(\x;\bm\mu_c,\Sigma_c)+\ln P(L=c)\Big],
\]
which, with uniform priors, reduces to choosing the largest class-conditional log-likelihood. Applied to the test set with the true pdfs, it gives
\[
\boxed{\hat P_{\text{opt}}(\text{error})=0.1484}
\]
This is the aspirational performance level for the MLPs. Its confusion counts are:

\subsection*{MLP model}
Each model is a two-layer MLP: $P$ hidden perceptrons with the smooth-ReLU (softplus) activation $\phi(a)=\ln(1+e^{a})$, followed by a softmax output layer:
\[
\mathbf h=\phi\big(W_1\tilde\x+\mathbf b_1\big)\in\mathbb R^P,\qquad
\hat P(L=c|\x)=y_c(\x)=\frac{\exp\big(\mathbf w_{2,c}^T\mathbf h+b_{2,c}\big)}{\sum_{k=1}^C\exp\big(\mathbf w_{2,k}^T\mathbf h+b_{2,k}\big)} .
\]
Here $\tilde\x$ is $\x$ standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the data the model is trained on, so validation and test data are never used for standardization. The model has $P(d+1)+C(P+1)=8P+4$ parameters $\bm\theta$. The softmax guarantees $y_c>0$ and $\sum_cy_c=1$.

\textbf{Maximum-likelihood training.} For iid training data $\{(\x_n,l_n)\}_{n=1}^N$ with one-hot labels $t_{nc}$, the likelihood of the labels under the model is $\prod_n\prod_c y_c(\x_n)^{t_{nc}}$. So the ML estimate minimizes the average cross-entropy
\[
\hat{\bm\theta}_{ML}=\argmin_{\bm\theta}\;-\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\sum_{c=1}^C t_{nc}\ln y_c(\x_n;\bm\theta).
\]
No regularization term is added, so this is pure ML as the question specifies. The objective is minimized with full-batch L-BFGS (\texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize}, method \texttt{L-BFGS-B}). The gradient is computed analytically by backpropagation: $\partial/\partial\mathbf a_2=(\mathbf y-\mathbf t)/N$ at the softmax, then back through $W_2$ and the softplus derivative $\phi'(a)=\sigma(a)=1/(1+e^{-a})$. Weights are initialized randomly (Glorot-style normal draws), with a fresh draw for every restart.

\textbf{Local optima.} The cross-entropy of an MLP is non-convex. Every training call therefore runs several independent random initializations and keeps the solution with the \emph{highest training log-likelihood} (lowest average cross-entropy). The final models use 10 restarts (up to 3000 L-BFGS iterations each). The cross-validation fits use 2 restarts (up to 1000 iterations each) to keep the computation manageable.

\textbf{Classification.} Treating $y_c(\x)$ as the class posterior, the MAP rule is $D(\x)=\argmax_c y_c(\x)$.

\subsection*{Model-order selection by 10-fold cross-validation}
For each training set, the candidate hidden-layer sizes were $P\in\{1,2,3,4,6,8,12,16,24,32\}$. The training set was shuffled and split into 10 disjoint folds (\texttt{sklearn.model\_selection.KFold}). For each $P$ and each fold, an MLP was trained by ML on the other 9 folds and used to classify the held-out fold. The CV objective is the total number of validation errors summed over the 10 folds, divided by $N$: an estimate of P(error) in which every sample is classified exactly once by a model that did not see it. The selected $P$ minimizes this estimate, with ties broken toward the smaller network. The 100 fits per training set (10 values of $P$ $\times$ 10 folds) ran in parallel with \texttt{joblib}.

\subsection*{Discussion}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Convergence to the optimum.} Test P(error) falls steadily with training-set size: 0.2508 at $N=100$, 0.1807 at $N=500$, 0.1523 at $N=1000$, 0.1501 at $N=5000$ and 0.1496 at $N=10000$, against 0.1484 for the true-pdf MAP classifier. The 95\% Wilson intervals (half-width $\approx0.0022$; see the figure) of the $N=5000$ and $N=10000$ models overlap the optimal classifier's interval. The trained MLPs therefore approach the Bayes decision rule, as expected of ML posterior estimates from a sufficiently flexible model.
\item \textbf{What cross-validation selects.} Very small networks underfit badly for every $N$: $P=1$ gives a CV error of about 0.45, because one hidden unit maps $\mathbb R^3$ onto a single scalar and cannot carve out four regions, and $P=2$ gives about 0.27. From $P=3$ upward the CV curve for large $N$ is essentially flat at the Bayes level. Three smooth ramps are already enough to approximate the four quadratic-boundary regions well, so the extra capacity neither helps nor hurts once data are plentiful. For small $N$ the curve is U-shaped: with 100 samples it rises from 0.18 at $P=3$ to 0.26--0.29 at larger $P$. Unregularized ML training of a large network on few points fits noise. The $N=100$ final model reaches a training error of 0.07 but a test error of 0.25.
\item \textbf{Selected sizes need not grow monotonically with $N$.} The selected $P$ is 3, 6, 3, 3, 12. For $N\ge1000$ the CV errors of all $P\ge3$ lie within about 0.005 of each other, comparable to the CV estimate's own sampling noise ($\sqrt{0.15\cdot0.85/N}\approx0.004$--$0.011$). Which $P$ wins is therefore partly chance; what matters is that every candidate $P\ge3$ performs at nearly the Bayes level. The $N=10000$ choice of $P=12$ (CV error 0.150, versus 0.153 for $P=3$) is consistent with more data supporting a slightly richer model, and its test error is the lowest of the five.
\item \textbf{Sources of the remaining gap.} At $N=100$ and $500$ the gap to optimal (0.10 and 0.03) is estimation error: too few samples to pin down 28--52 parameters by unpenalized ML. At $N\ge5000$ the remaining gap of at most 0.0017 is within test-set sampling uncertainty. The training sets are independent draws rather than nested subsets, so the learning curve is not guaranteed to be monotone, though here it is.
\end{itemize}


\subsection*{How the software was verified}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gradient.} The MLP and its backpropagation were written directly in NumPy, so the gradient was checked against a central finite-difference approximation (\texttt{scipy.optimize.approx\_fprime}) at a random parameter vector. The relative error was $4.4\times10^{-7}$, confirming that L-BFGS receives the exact gradient of the cross-entropy it minimizes.
\item \textbf{Outputs.} The softmax outputs were checked to be positive and to sum to 1 for every sample. The log-softmax is computed by subtracting the row maximum, and softplus as \texttt{logaddexp(0,a)}, so neither overflows for large activations.
\item \textbf{Objective.} The loss is exactly the average negative log-likelihood of the one-hot labels under the softmax outputs, i.e.\ the ML criterion. No weight decay or early stopping is applied, and every L-BFGS run starts from a new random initialization.
\item \textbf{Cross-validation.} The code asserts that the 10 validation folds are disjoint from their training folds and together cover every training sample exactly once. Standardization statistics are recomputed inside each training call, so no validation data leak into training.
\item \textbf{Test set.} The 100{,}000-sample test set is used only after model selection and final training, never for choosing $P$ or for stopping.
\item \textbf{Optimizer convergence.} The L-BFGS status of every restart was recorded by re-running the final fits and all cross-validation fits with their original seeds. The script asserts that every error count reproduces exactly. Here ``converged'' means the projected gradient fell below $10^{-7}$ before the iteration cap, a deliberately strict tolerance.

\section*{Question 2: GMM model-order selection with cross-validation}

\subsection*{True GMM}
The true density is $p(\x)=\sum_{k=1}^4\alpha_k\,\mathcal N(\x;\bm\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$, $\x\in\mathbb R^2$, with
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}\toprule
$k$ & $\alpha_k$ & $\bm\mu_k$ & $\Sigma_k$ & mean eigenvalue of $\Sigma_k$\\\midrule
1 & 0.30 & $[0,\,0]^T$ & $\begin{bmatrix}1.0&0.3\\0.3&0.8\end{bmatrix}$ & 0.90\\[8pt]
2 & 0.25 & $[1.6,\,0.6]^T$ & $\begin{bmatrix}0.7&-0.2\\-0.2&1.0\end{bmatrix}$ & 0.85\\[8pt]
3 & 0.25 & $[6,\,4]^T$ & $\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.6\\0.6&0.9\end{bmatrix}$ & 1.20\\[8pt]
4 & 0.20 & $[-5,\,5]^T$ & $\begin{bmatrix}0.6&0\\0&1.8\end{bmatrix}$ & 1.20\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
All four components have different weights, means and covariances. Components 1 and 2 overlap strongly: their means are $\|\bm\mu_1-\bm\mu_2\|=1.71$ apart, while the sum of their average covariance eigenvalues is $1.75$, the same order as the question suggests. Together they form a single elongated lump. Components 3 and 4 are well separated from everything else.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/q2_data.png}
\caption{True GMM density contours with one dataset of each size, colored by the (hidden) generating component.}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Procedure}
For each repetition $r=1,\dots,R$ with $R=100$, and each size $N\in\{10,100,1000\}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Draw a fresh iid dataset of $N$ samples from the true GMM.
\item Shuffle it and split it into 10 disjoint folds. For $N=10$ each fold holds one sample, so this is leave-one-out.
\item For each candidate order $K=1,\dots,10$ and each fold, fit a $K$-component, full-covariance GMM to the other 9 folds by maximum likelihood with the EM algorithm (scikit-learn's \texttt{GaussianMixture}). Then compute the log-likelihood of the held-out fold under the fitted model, $\sum_{\x\in\text{fold}}\ln\hat p_K(\x)$.
\item Sum the held-out log-likelihoods over the 10 folds; dividing by $N$ gives the average validation log-likelihood $\overline{\ell}_K$. Every sample is scored exactly once by a model that did not see it.
\item Select $\hat K=\argmax_K\overline{\ell}_K$.
\end{enumerate}
The selection rate of order $K$ at size $N$ is the fraction of the $R$ repetitions with $\hat K=K$. Both parameter estimation (EM maximizes training log-likelihood) and validation (held-out log-likelihood) use the log-likelihood of the data, as the question requires.

\textbf{EM settings and why.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Local optima.} Each EM fit runs 3 k-means++ initializations (\texttt{n\_init=3}) and keeps the highest-likelihood solution, with up to 500 iterations and tolerance $10^{-4}$.
\item \emph{Covariance floor.} The GMM likelihood is unbounded: a component can collapse onto a single training point with covariance $\to0$. To keep the ML problem well posed, $10^{-3}I$ is added to every covariance estimate (\texttt{reg\_covar}). This is negligible compared with the true covariances, whose eigenvalues are 0.5--1.9. A collapsed component still wastes probability mass on held-out points, so CV still penalizes overfitting.
\item \emph{Infeasible orders.} A $K$-component GMM cannot be fitted to fewer than $K$ points. For $N=10$ each training split has 9 samples, so $K=10$ is assigned validation log-likelihood $-\infty$ and can never be selected. This is a real limitation of 10 samples, not a software artifact.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Discussion}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$N=10$: cross-validation picks the simplest model (K=1 in 93\% of repetitions).} Each training split has only nine points, and component 4 has on average only two samples in the whole dataset. A two-dimensional full-covariance Gaussian has 5 free parameters, so every extra component multiplies the estimation variance. Extra components typically lock onto one or two training points with tiny covariances, and the held-out point then falls far from them. The median validation log-likelihood falls from $-6.0$ at $K=1$ to $-81$ at $K=4$ and below $-1000$ at $K\ge8$, more than two orders of magnitude worse than the true density's $-3.90$. A single broad Gaussian covering all three clusters is the best predictive model this little data can support. Ten samples carry essentially no information about the true order.
\item \textbf{$N=100$: the two overlapping components are merged (K=3 in 92\%).} With 100 samples the three \emph{visible} clusters are well estimated, which explains the jump in validation log-likelihood from $K=2$ to $K=3$. Components 1 and 2, however, have means only $1.71$ apart, comparable to the sum of their average eigenvalues ($0.90+0.85=1.75$). Their combined density is a single unimodal, slightly elongated lump, and roughly 55 points in it are not enough to estimate the 6 extra parameters a split requires. In 92 of 100 repetitions the 4-component model scored lower on held-out data than the 3-component model; the median difference was $-0.065$ nats per sample. The bias of merging the pair costs less than the variance of separating it.
\item \textbf{$N=1000$: the true order dominates (K=4 in 83\%).} With roughly 550 points in the overlapping pair, the 4-component model's better fit outweighs its extra variance. $K=4$ beat $K=3$ on held-out likelihood in 97\% of repetitions, by a median of $0.013$ nats per sample, and the median CV curve peaks at $K=4$ ($-3.918$), within $0.02$ of the true-density value $-3.899$. The remaining selections mostly lean toward \emph{over}fitting ($K=5$: 13\%, $K=6$: 3\%). The validation curve beyond $K=4$ is very flat (median drop of only $0.004$ from $K=4$ to $5$), because a superfluous component can sit almost redundantly inside an existing one at little cost. With that little separation, sampling noise sometimes tips the choice upward. Underfitting ($K=3$) occurs only once.
\item \textbf{Overall pattern.} The selected order rises with the amount of data (mean $\hat K$: 1.08, 3.08, 4.18). Cross-validated log-likelihood estimates the model with the best \emph{predictive} density for the data at hand, not the number of generating components. With limited data it deliberately prefers simpler models, and it recovers the true structure only once the data can resolve it. The overlapping pair is exactly where the true order is hardest to identify, and it accounts for nearly all the $K=3$ choices at $N=100$.
\item \textbf{Statistical precision.} Each rate is a proportion out of $R=100$ independent repetitions, with standard error $\sqrt{r(1-r)/100}\le0.05$. For example, the $N=1000$ rate for $K=4$ is $0.83\pm0.04$. The ranking of the most frequent order at every $N$ is therefore well beyond Monte Carlo noise. Rates of 0\% mean ``not observed in 100 trials'', not ``impossible''.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity to settings.} The covariance floor ($10^{-3}I$) and the 3 EM restarts are part of the fitted model family. A larger floor would soften the large-$K$ collapse at $N=10$; the conclusions at $N=100$ and $N=1000$ come from well-populated components, whose covariance eigenvalues are hundreds of times larger than the floor, so they are not driven by it.
\end{itemize}
